I. Introduction

Species-level traits are a central focus in ecology because they influence species fitness (Bergamo, Traveset, and Lázaro 2021), regulate population dynamics (Herrera 2005), and even shape evolutionary trajectories (Cosmo et al. 2023). These traits structure interactions within plant–pollinator networks, which is important because mutualistic networks provide essential ecosystem services in both natural and agricultural systems (Waser 2006). Network theory predicts that pairwise interactions change through space. Highly dynamic pairwise interactions complicates our ability to generalize plant species’ network roles across a landscape. Examining partner variability as a plat trait, and how this trait influences network niche through space can help inform conservation planning of rare, cultural, and restoration valuable plants.

Mutualistic networks consist of two interacting communities called nodes that are connected by links. Using a bipartite approach, we can map species interactions and identify species roles within a network. Well-characterized pairwise interactions change spatially and temporally (L. W. Aarssen 1989; L. Aarssen 1992; Bengtsson, Fagerström, and Rydin 1994; Carstensen et al. 2014). This variation is driven by temporal variation in species composition and phenology (Alarcón, Waser, and Ollerton 2008; Olesen et al. 2008) and interaction re-wiring due to disturbance (Ponisio 2020) or resource availability (Valdovinos et al. 2016).

An important aspect of species diversity is beta diversity–the spatial turnover of species interactions (Carstensen et al. 2014). The dissimilarity in beta-diversity of populations in a region is low if populations are interacting with the same species compositions and high when interacting with unique compositions (Ponisio 2020). Plant species exhibit substantial variability in their interaction partners among conspecific individuals within a population (Gómez, Perfectti, and Jordano 2011), across populations (González, Murúa, and Pérez 2015), and among communities (Herrera 2005). Flexible pairwise interactions likely contribute to a plant species ability to shift between network niches when community composition changes. Thus, it is valuable to examine whether species that are flexible in their interaction partners can occupy different as community composition changes through space.

This study focuses on prairies in the Willamette Valley ecoregion in Oregon. Over the past 175 years native prairie ecosystems in the Pacific Northwest have suffered a drastic decline, with approximately 90-98% lost throughout the region (Sinclair et al. 2006; Altman 2011). Advancing our understanding of interaction-based ecosystem services by treating partner variability as a trait and evaluating its impacts on population dynamics and network niches is essential for informing conservation planning of these imperiled ecosystems. Yet, studies that link partner variability and network niche remains scarce (see Ponisio (2020)). Here, I assess plant partner variability as a trait, and how this trait may predict population dynamics and network niches.

Across populations plants show substantial variation in their interaction partners. This is likely explained by shifting compositions of available interaction partners as community compositions change with geographic distance (Carstensen et al. 2014). As plants become more flexible in their interaction partners, their interannual population dynamics are likely to be less variable because they can more reliably obtain necessary pollination services. Moreover, the network roles they occupy may shift accordingly. This flexible behavior has been observed in plant populations of L. latifolia (Herrera 2005), and pollinator populations (Ponisio 2020; Gaiarsa, Kremen, and Ponisio 2021). These insights have motivated three hypothesis: (1) As beta-diversity increases, the role of a plant within the network will shift. (2) As plants become more flexible in their interaction partners they will show more variation in their network niche (3) Plants that are more flexible in their interaction partners will have a negative relationship with changes in floral abundance among years.

II. Data

Data was provided by our collaborator Susan Waters with Quamash EcoResearch. This dataset included plant-pollinator interactions across 10 sites within the Willamette Valley Ecoregion in Oregon from 2019-2022. The data was collected using standardized timed hand-netting survey methods where bees are collected when actively engaging in pollination. The interacting plant species partners were recorded in all observations and identified to species. Bee species were identified as morphospecies or species when feasible. Site and date (survey round) were recorded for all observations. Using the raw data I created a vegetation dataset that included all plant species sampled and their abundances for each survey round within a site and year, a network dataset that included all plant pollinator interactions, and a spatial dataset with the latitude and longitude converted to a vector (see below). The preparation of datasets and the development of the analytical approach was based on the steps used in Ponisio (2020). The code was sourced from github and modified to meet the analytical destination in the present study.

V. Beta Diversity

To quantify how plants are flexible in their interaction partners across populations, I calculated Beta diversity—the change in interaction partners among plant populations across space within a single year (2partner.R). Using the observed communities and the null communities I calculated beta diversity constrained by alpha diversity using bipartite:betadisper, and then quantified the dissimilarity across populations within a year using the Chao method. Beta-diversity was only calculated for species that were observed at least three times within a single year. The output dataset included beta-diversity values for each species at a site within a single year, and these values were used to represent plant partner variability.

VI. Network Niche Variability

I calculated plant network roles and a principal component analysis (PCA) to later be used as my response variables regressed against partner variability (3pca.R). I used the network and specimen dataset and applied bipartite::specieslevel function to calculate pollinator and plant network roles. I subsetted the data to only include plant species roles and dropped pollinators. I selected PCA loadings of interest which encompass a species network role (i.e., rare degree, weighted betweenness, weighted closeness, niche overlap, species strength, ) and calculated the mean and variance of the PCA scores. The output dataset was a list of PCA scores for each plant species at a site within a single year. I chose to use the variance of the PCA scores to represent a plant’s ability to occupy different network niches across populations in the landscape because variance better captures the spread of the data than the would otherwise be lost if using the mean.

IX. Linear Models

To test my hypotheses I created linear models (6modelPlot.R). I first visualized each variable of interest using a histogram to see any extreme outliers. I created four separate linear models. Each model had a different response variable, partner variability (beta-diversity) was a fixed effect, and I included site as a random effect. I created a linear model for each of the following response variables: log-ratio floral abundance, d, nested rank, and network niche variability (i.e., using PCA variance). Example linear model: network niche variability ~ scale(beta-diversity) + (1|Site). I then used the predicted coefficients from each model to plot the results.

plot.s <- function(specAnalysis, response, mod) {
  # Create prediction grid
  expand.grid <- data.frame(
    dist = seq(min(specAnalysis$dist),
               max(specAnalysis$dist),
               length.out = 100)
  )
  
  # Add placeholder column for response
  expand.grid[[response]] <- 0
  
  # Predict using custom function
  pred <- predict.int(mod = mod,
                      dd = expand.grid,
                      y = response,
                      family = "gaussian")
  
  # Plot
  ggplot() +
    # geom_point(data = pred, aes(x = dist, y = .data[[response]])) +
    geom_line(data = pred, aes(x = dist, y = .data[[response]]), color = "blue", linewidth = 1.2) +
    geom_ribbon(data = pred, aes(x = dist, ymin = plo, ymax = phi), alpha = 0.2, fill = "blue") +
    theme_classic() +
    theme(text = element_text(size = 20))
}

X. Conclusions

Partner variability as a plant trait proves to be a fruitful area of study. Plants were flexible in their interaction partners across space, with comparable expected partner variability for those sites with high sampling effort. This is likely explained by communities with similar pairwise interactions locally, as the communities sampled in the present study are close in geographic area (Carstensen et al. 2014).

Plant variability was positively associated with network niche variability, whereas its associations with d and nested rank were not statistically significant. These patterns indicate that plants with flexible interaction partners may maintain similar roles within a network while also influencing the overall network structure. Gaiarsa, Kremen, and Ponisio (2021) performed a similar study from the pollinators perspective using a motif approach to quantify role variability incorporating indirect interactions. They found that role variability and partner variability were not related. I propose that differences in the structural organization of interaction partners between plants and pollinators cause each group to influence network structure in distinct ways, likely reflecting the different benefits they derive from mutualistic interactions (Van Der Kooi, Vallejo-Marı́n, and Leonhardt 2021).

Interestingly, partner variability was negatively associated with differences in floral abundance across pairwise years, although this relationship was not statistically significant. Floral abundance is linked to network asymmetry: abundant species tend to exert stronger interaction effects on their partners than they receive in return, whereas the opposite pattern is observed for rare species (Vázquez et al. 2007). In addition, the likelihood that a pollinator interacts with a particular plant is influenced by variation in floral abundance (Carstensen et al. 2014). In our case, from the plant’s perspective, changes in pairwise interactions did not affect floral abundance.
